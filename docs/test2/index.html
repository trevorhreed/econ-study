Price Controls

In a free market, price and quantity are determined by the intersection of supply and demand. Governments sometimes override this with legal price limits. There are two types:

A is a legal maximum. It's only binding (i.e., actually affects the market) if set below equilibrium.

What happens when it binds:

At the artificially low price, quantity demanded rises but quantity supplied falls → . The gap between them is the shortage.

Short run vs. long run: In the short run, both supply and demand are relatively , so the shortage is small. In the long run, both curves become more — buyers find more reasons to buy cheap goods, sellers increasingly exit — and the shortage widens dramatically.

Side effects: Rationing problems (who gets the scarce good?), black markets, quality deterioration, discrimination by sellers.

Classic example: Rent control. Short-term, landlords keep renting; long-term, they stop maintaining buildings, convert to condos, and new construction collapses.

If you cap the price of something below what it costs to produce, eventually nobody will produce it.

A is a legal minimum. Binding only if set above equilibrium.

When it binds: Quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded → .

Classic example: Minimum wage. At above-equilibrium wages, firms want fewer workers but more people want jobs → unemployment (a surplus of labor). Low-skill workers are hit hardest — they're the ones priced out entirely.

Agricultural price supports are another example: the government sets a minimum crop price and often must buy the surplus to maintain it.

If you force the price of something above what buyers think it's worth, you'll create a pile of unsold goods (or unemployed workers).

Taxation

A tax creates a between the price buyers pay (P_b) and the price sellers receive (P_s). The difference equals the tax: P_b − P_s = tax.

P_buyers − P_sellers = Tax Amount

Key insight: It does not matter whether the tax is legally imposed on buyers or sellers. The market outcome — prices, quantities, and burden — is identical either way. Statutory incidence (who writes the check) ≠ economic incidence (who actually bears the cost).

Mechanism (tax on sellers): Supply shifts up by the tax amount. New equilibrium has lower quantity, higher buyer price, lower seller net price.

Mechanism (tax on buyers): Demand shifts down by the tax amount. Same result.

Think of the tax as a wall between buyers and sellers. It doesn't matter which side you build it on — the wall is the same size and creates the same gap.

Deadweight loss (DWL) = the destroyed because the tax reduces the quantity traded. Trades that would have been mutually beneficial no longer happen.

Graphically: Triangle between the supply curve, demand curve, and the new (reduced) quantity line.

Critical relationship: DWL grows with the square of the tax rate. Double the tax → roughly 4× the DWL. This is because larger taxes cause disproportionately larger reductions in quantity.

DWL ≈ ½ × tax × ΔQ (triangle)

DWL is larger when supply and/or demand are more (more responsive → bigger quantity reduction).

DWL is smaller when curves are (quantity barely changes → small triangle).

The tax burden falls more on whichever side of the market is more .

Why: The inelastic side can't easily escape the market. If demand is inelastic (buyers need the good no matter what), buyers absorb most of the tax. If supply is inelastic (sellers are locked in), sellers absorb most.

Extreme cases:

— Perfectly inelastic demand → buyers bear 100%, no DWL, no quantity change.

— Perfectly elastic supply → buyers bear 100% of the tax; sellers escape entirely.

The side that can't leave gets stuck with the bill.

As tax rate rises from 0%, revenue initially increases. But at very high rates, the tax base shrinks (quantity traded collapses) and revenue falls.

At 0%: revenue = 0. At 100%: revenue ≈ 0 (nobody trades). Maximum revenue is somewhere in between.

This is the logic — higher rates don't always mean more revenue.

Welfare Economics

asks: how does the allocation of resources affect economic well-being? The tools: , , and .

Consumer surplus (CS) = willingness to pay minus actual price, summed across all buyers.

Willingness to pay is the maximum a buyer would pay for a good. It's captured by the demand curve — each point on the demand curve represents a buyer's (or a unit's) willingness to pay.

CS = Area below demand curve, above price, from 0 to Q*

What changes CS: A price decrease increases CS (existing buyers get a bigger bonus, and new buyers enter). A price increase does the opposite.

CS measures the collective "deal" buyers are getting. If you'd pay $80 for headphones but they cost $50, your surplus is $30. Aggregate that across every buyer.

Producer surplus (PS) = price received minus cost of production, summed across all sellers.

Cost here means the minimum a seller would accept — captured by the supply curve.

PS = Area above supply curve, below price, from 0 to Q*

Higher prices increase PS; lower prices decrease it.

If it costs you $30 to make a widget and you sell it for $50, your surplus is $20. The supply curve shows the "bare minimum" each seller would accept.

= CS + PS. It measures the total net benefit to society from trade in a market.

Total Surplus = CS + PS = (Value to buyers) − (Cost to sellers)

An allocation is efficient if it maximizes total surplus. The free-market achieves this — any quantity above or below the equilibrium quantity would reduce total surplus.

Why equilibrium is efficient:

— For every unit up to Q*, the buyer's value (demand) exceeds the seller's cost (supply) → trade creates surplus.

— For units beyond Q*, cost exceeds value → producing them destroys surplus.

— So producing exactly Q* maximizes total surplus.

The free market is like a machine that automatically produces every trade worth making and stops exactly where trades stop being worth it.

The result that free markets maximize total surplus requires assumptions: no externalities (costs/benefits spilling onto third parties), no (no one firm controlling price), and full information.

When these fail, markets produce the wrong quantity, and results. Chapters on externalities and monopoly build on this.

Government intervention (taxes, price controls, regulation) can potentially improve efficiency in the presence of market failure — but can also make things worse if poorly designed.

Evaluating Price Controls & Taxes Through the Efficiency Lens

A binding or moves the market away from the efficient quantity. Trades that would have created surplus don't happen.

Price ceiling: Quantity traded falls to quantity supplied (the short side). CS may rise for those who get the good, but total surplus falls because too few units are produced.

Price floor: Quantity traded falls to quantity demanded. PS may rise for sellers who sell, but total surplus falls because too many resources go into production that buyers don't want at that price.

Both controls create deadweight loss for the same reason: they prevent mutually beneficial trades from happening.

Taxes reduce quantity traded, creating . The tax revenue is a transfer (from buyers/sellers to government), not a loss — it can still be used. But the DWL triangle represents pure waste: trades that would have made both sides better off, gone.

Efficient taxation minimizes DWL per dollar of revenue raised. This means taxing goods with demand/supply (where quantity won't change much). This is the logic behind taxing cigarettes, gasoline, and land.

What Are Costs?

Explicit costs: Out-of-pocket payments to inputs (wages, rent, materials). What accountants track.

Implicit costs: Opportunity costs of resources the firm owns. Example: if you run your own business, your implicit cost includes the salary you gave up by not working elsewhere.

Economic Profit = Revenue − (Explicit Costs + Implicit Costs) Accounting Profit = Revenue − Explicit Costs

Economic profit is always ≤ accounting profit. A firm with zero economic profit is still earning a "normal return" — enough to keep the owner from leaving.

Economic cost asks: "What did you sacrifice to do this?" If you use your own building, the cost isn't $0 — it's the rent someone would have paid you. Production & Costs

The production function maps inputs → output. As you add more of one input (holding others fixed), output increases — but at a decreasing rate. This is .

Example: One worker bakes 50 cookies. Two workers bake 90 (not 100). Three bake 120. Each additional worker adds less than the previous.

Why it matters for costs: Diminishing returns mean each additional unit of output costs more to produce. This is why the MC curve eventually slopes upward.

If you keep adding cooks to one kitchen, eventually they're bumping into each other. Each new cook contributes less than the last. The Cost Curves

Fixed costs (FC): Don't vary with output. Rent, equipment leases, salaried managers. Present even at Q = 0.

Variable costs (VC): Change with output. Raw materials, hourly labor, electricity for production.

TC = FC + VC

The total cost curve starts at FC (when Q = 0) and rises at an increasing rate (because of diminishing returns).

 ATC = TC / Q = AFC + AVC AFC = FC / Q   (always declining) AVC = VC / Q   (eventually rising)

The ATC U-shape: At low Q, ATC is high because you're spreading fixed costs over very few units (AFC is large). As Q rises, AFC falls and ATC drops. But eventually kick in, AVC rises fast enough to dominate, and ATC starts rising.

The bottom of the ATC curve is the — the quantity that minimizes per-unit cost.

Marginal cost (MC) = the cost of producing one additional unit.

MC = ΔTC / ΔQ

MC and ATC relationship (critical):

When MC < ATC → ATC is falling (producing the next unit is cheaper than average, pulling average down).

When MC > ATC → ATC is rising (next unit costs more than average, pulling average up).

Therefore: MC crosses ATC at ATC's minimum.

Think of your GPA. If your next semester's grades (MC) are higher than your cumulative GPA (ATC), your GPA rises. If lower, it falls. They're equal at the turning point.

Short Run vs. Long Run

Short run: At least one input is fixed (typically capital — factory size, equipment). Firm can only adjust variable inputs.

Long run: All inputs are variable. Firm can change factory size, relocate, restructure entirely.

The long-run ATC curve is an envelope of all possible short-run ATC curves — for each output level, the firm picks the plant size that minimizes cost.

: Long-run ATC falls as output increases. Causes: specialization, bulk purchasing, spreading large fixed costs.

Constant returns to scale: Long-run ATC stays flat.

: Long-run ATC rises as output increases. Causes: coordination problems, bureaucracy, communication breakdowns in large organizations.

In the short run, you're stuck with your kitchen. In the long run, you can build a bigger kitchen — or a smaller one — optimized for whatever quantity you want to produce.

Why Monopolies Exist

A = single seller, no close substitutes, high barriers to entry. Three main sources:

1. Exclusive resource ownership: Rare. Classic (dated) example: De Beers and diamonds.

2. Government-created monopolies: Patents, copyrights, licenses. Government grants exclusive rights, usually to incentivize innovation.

3. Natural monopoly: A arises when a single firm can serve the entire market at lower ATC than multiple firms could. This happens when there are very large relative to — the ATC curve is still declining at market-relevant quantities. Examples: utilities, infrastructure (water, electricity grid).

It makes no sense to build two competing sewer systems in one city. The fixed cost is enormous, and once built, the cost per household drops with every connection. One system is cheaper than two. Monopoly Decision-Making

Unlike a competitive firm (), a monopolist faces the entire market demand curve. To sell more, it must lower price — not just on the extra unit, but on all units.

This means MR < Price for every unit after the first.

MR = Price + (ΔP × Q_existing)

The second term is negative (price drops on all existing units), so MR is always less than demand/price. On a linear demand curve, the MR curve has the same intercept but twice the slope.

Imagine you sell 10 widgets at $10 = $100 revenue. To sell 11, you cut price to $9.50 for ALL units. Revenue = 11 × $9.50 = $104.50. MR of the 11th unit = $4.50, not $9.50. You gained $9.50 from the new unit but lost $0.50 × 10 on existing units.

The monopolist maximizes profit where MR = MC. This gives the profit-maximizing quantity.

Then the firm reads up to the demand curve to find the price consumers will pay for that quantity.

1. Find Q where MR = MC 2. Price = Demand(Q) — read price off the demand curve 3. Profit = (P − ATC) × Q

Key: The monopolist does NOT produce where MC = Demand. That would be the competitive (efficient) outcome. The monopolist produces less and charges more.

The Cost of Monopoly

Because the monopolist restricts output below the efficient level, some mutually beneficial trades don't happen. The is the triangle between demand, MC, from monopoly Q to competitive Q.

Redistribution: Some of what would have been is transferred to the monopolist as profit (the rectangle of markup × quantity). This is a redistribution, not a pure loss. But the DWL triangle is pure waste.

The monopolist's problem isn't that they charge too much per se — it's that they produce too little. The high price is a symptom of restricted output.

= charging different prices to different buyers based on willingness to pay. Types:

Perfect (1st degree): Charge each buyer their max willingness to pay. Captures ALL surplus — CS goes to zero, no DWL (efficient quantity is produced). Theoretical ideal, rarely achieved.

2nd degree: Price varies by quantity purchased (bulk discounts, tiered pricing).

3rd degree: Different prices for different groups (student discounts, senior pricing, geographic pricing).

Requirements: Firm must have market power, must be able to identify willingness to pay, and must prevent resale (arbitrage).

Price discrimination can actually increase total welfare by getting output closer to the efficient level — though the surplus goes to the monopolist, not consumers. Policy Responses

Antitrust law: Prevent mergers that would create monopolies; break up existing ones; prohibit anticompetitive practices (price fixing, predatory pricing).

Regulation: Government sets the price. Options:

— Marginal-cost pricing (P = MC): Efficient but may cause losses for natural monopolies (since MC < ATC when ATC is declining).

— Average-cost pricing (P = ATC): Firm breaks even but quantity is still below the efficient level.

Public ownership: Government runs the monopoly directly. Mixed track record — government enterprises often face poor incentives and inefficiency.

Do nothing: Sometimes the cure is worse than the disease, especially if the market is contestable (potential competitors discipline the monopolist even without entry).
